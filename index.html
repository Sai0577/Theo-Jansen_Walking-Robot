01 / Project overview

A simple idea: turning rotation into walking.

The Theo Jansen walking mechanism is a linkage-based leg system. In this project, a compact prototype is made using easily available materials. A DC gear motor supplies rotary input, while the connected ice-cream-stick links move around their pivot pins and produce a walking-like motion at the feet.

01 — ABSTRACT

Project abstract

This project demonstrates a motor-driven walking robot inspired by the leg mechanism developed by Dutch kinetic artist and engineer Theo Jansen. The model uses a cardboard body, ice-cream sticks, connecting pins, a DC gear motor, a battery and a switch. Motor rotation drives the linkage, causing the legs to move in a coordinated stepping cycle.

02 — PROBLEM STATEMENT

Problem statement

Wheeled robots can find it difficult to move over some uneven or obstructed surfaces. This project explores a mechanical alternative: using linked legs to create a stepping movement from a simple rotary motor input.

03 — AIM

Aim of the project

To design and demonstrate a low-cost walking robot that converts the rotary motion of a DC gear motor into a repeated leg movement using a linkage mechanism.

Objectives

  • Understand the working of a multi-link walking mechanism.
  • Convert rotary motion into an approximate walking path.
  • Build a functional prototype using simple, low-cost materials.
  • Observe the role of links, pivot joints and motor speed in movement.
  • Develop practical skills in assembly, testing and mechanical problem-solving.
Project scope
This is an educational prototype inspired by the Theo Jansen principle. The exact link lengths and geometry of the original Strandbeest are not claimed for this model; the prototype demonstrates the basic conversion of rotary input into leg motion.
02 / Engineering principle

How does the robot walk?

A motor rotates a crank or driving connection. The connected bars transmit this motion through the linkage. Because the bars are joined by pivot pins, their positions continuously change and the foot follows a loop-like path: it lifts, swings forward, touches the ground and moves backward relative to the body during the support phase.

Working sequence

  1. Electrical input: The battery supplies electrical energy to the motor when the switch is turned on.
  2. Rotary input: The DC gear motor rotates its output shaft at reduced speed and increased usable torque compared with a high-speed motor.
  3. Link movement: The rotating drive connection moves the connected links around their pivot joints.
  4. Foot path: The linkage guides the leg through a repeated lift-and-step cycle.
  5. Forward movement: As the feet grip the floor during the support part of the cycle, the body is pushed forward.

Link

A rigid member that connects two or more joints and transmits motion or force.

Pivot joint

A connection that allows two links to rotate relative to each other around a pin.

Crank

A rotating member that provides the input motion to the linkage mechanism.

Important: The original Jansen leg uses carefully selected link proportions to create a near-flat ground-contact part of the foot path. Our classroom model is a simplified, handmade version, so its motion and efficiency depend on the accuracy of its joints, link lengths and alignment.

04 / Fabrication process

From individual sticks to a walking model.

The following sequence describes the general assembly process for the photographed prototype. Adjustments may be made during testing to reduce friction and improve balance.

Prepare the body frame

Cut and assemble the cardboard to create a central body that can support the motor, battery and leg mechanisms.

Prepare the links

Mark the required joint positions on the ice-cream sticks and make pivot holes carefully. Keep corresponding links consistent in length where the design requires it.

Assemble the leg linkages

Connect the sticks using pins. The joints should be secure but free enough to rotate without excessive friction.

Mount the motor

Fix the DC gear motor to the body and connect its rotating output to the driving part of the linkage.

Connect the power circuit

Connect the battery, switch and motor in a suitable circuit. Check polarity and secure exposed wire connections.

Test and adjust

Switch on the motor and observe the leg cycle. Correct loose pins, rubbing links, poor alignment or an unstable body before repeated operation.

06 / Learning and applications

What can this mechanism teach us?

Legged robotics research

Linkage-based legs are studied as alternatives to wheels in robots that need stepping motion.

Educational demonstrations

The mechanism helps students understand kinematics, linkages, joints, motion conversion and prototyping.

Walking models and kinetic art

The same principle inspires mechanical toys, moving sculptures and experimental walking machines.

Advantages

  • Uses a simple rotary motor as the main drive input.
  • Demonstrates motion conversion using mechanical links.
  • Can be made with inexpensive and easily available materials.
  • Provides hands-on experience in design, assembly and testing.

Limitations

  • Cardboard and ice-cream sticks have limited strength and durability.
  • Loose or misaligned joints can reduce smooth movement.
  • The model may need a flat surface and careful balancing.
  • It is a demonstration prototype, not a load-carrying industrial robot.

Conclusion
The Theo Jansen Walking Robot project demonstrates how a simple rotary input from a DC gear motor can be converted into a repeated stepping movement through interconnected links. By building and testing the prototype, the team gained practical understanding of linkages, pivot joints, motion transmission, basic electrical connections and mechanical fabrication. The project also shows how a low-cost model can be used to explore an interesting concept in walking robotics.
